There is no unified curriculum for Digital Humanities (DH) computing and, given the discipline's broad scope the richness born of the diversity of its instructors, the DH community have been wary of adopting restrictive resources. In the case of teaching computing, however, the lack of pedagogical resources can be a challenge. DH instructors may look to borrow curriculum from Computer Science, but that curriculum is founded in a different epistemological tradition — one with values and priorities that sometimes contradict 'humanistic' ones. To be able to teach students to do computing in a way that is aligned with humanistic inquiry, we need DH-specific computing education resources.

This digital · humanities computing competency framework is an attempt to provide instructors with a scaffold for defining competencies that are tailored to their content and instructional context but are also oriented toward a humanistic paradigm computing.

The framework has three main components: activities, literacies, and dispositions.

Knowledge Creation Activities

The competency frameowrk structured around a set of activities that are each defined by a pair of terms taken respectively from breakdowns of humanistic scholarly primitives (Unsworth, Pacheco) and of computing activities/computational thinking (CS2023, Hsu, Shute, Selby). They bring together elements of knowledge creation and validation that carry similarities in their purpose, but may go about it in different ways, in line with the divergent epistemological traditions of computing and the humanities.

These are associated with the two types of tasks one might undertake in DH computational work — creation and analysis — but ideally these should be happening simultaneously, working toward an ideal of critical making where they are one in the same.

DH Computing Literacies

Because of the frequent DH focus of creative expression, it can be useful to think of competence in terms of literacy — a literacy tied to the medium of computing, rather than text.

In the context of the framework, there are three layers to this DH computing literacy; these are the different levels at which learners should be able understand/explain their work. They are crucially not a scale of increasing competency, as featured in many competency frameworks, but, rather, dimensions of competency.

Like the activities, they are defined by paired terms and bring together computing and humanities sources — notably ideas of layered literacies for communication (DiSessa, Cook) and models of programming composition and comprehension (Schulte, Cutts).

The three dimensions of this DH Computing Literacy are:

  • Mechanical · Mediatory: CS notions of hardware and computational representation meet humanities interests in medium specificity and the impact of mediation.
  • Procedural · Relational: CS specification of process anchored in determinism and consensus seeking meets networked humanities procedures based in scholarship as conversation
  • Functional · Rhetorical: The practical CS question of what does it do, meets the more abstract humanities questions of why does it do and what is it saying by doing.

The aim of using the sets of paired terms throughout is twofold. First, it highlights and invites interrogation of the ways different disciplines view knowledge production processes and the friction between them, which is a foundational consideration of DH computing. Second, it serves to create some degree of familiarity for instructors coming from different disciplinary traditions, facilitating the creation of connections that could help them leverage and adapt existing pedagogical skills.

The aim is not to represent the terms (and by extension, the disciplines) as opposed or irreconcilable but, rather, as inclusive, non-hierarchical and generative aspects of knowledge production. To this end, they are typographically represented using the interpunct, rather than something like a slash or pipe which, in a CS context, are often associated with binary ‘or’. The interpunct, in contrast, is used in the French écriture inclusive — a movement which is in part about questioning/pushing back against the (masculine) default in the language in a way that is similar to how DH questions/rethinks computational norms.

Humanistic Computing Dispositions

Competencies are made up of skills, knowledge, and dispositions. Typically, dispositions are defined in terms of desired behaviours or qualities of a competent practitioner. This framework takes a slightly different apporach, identifying diapositions with regard to a specific type of practice/a theoretical ideal: the high-level aim in DH of finding a way of doing computing that aligns with the values and priorities of humanistic inquiry. A sample of instructors described three core elements of this paradigmn of 'humanistic computing'

  • Human History, Human Future: Humanistic computing should be a practice in which computing is both situated in the long history of the human experience and continually used to help understand what it means to be human. This means situating computing in a long history of media and technology and not seeing technology only as an influence on society, but a product of it. Looking forward, it means interrogating the impact of society becoming increasingly digitised on human identity.
  • Plurality and Complexity: he power of computation is drawn from its facility for information processing at a speed and scale beyond human capability. Prerequisite to this is normalisation and abstraction that would seem to stand in opposition to the plurality and complexity valued in humanistic inquiry, which is always interested in seeing things from a multiplicity of perspectives. Humanistic computing should be about questioning and reorienting the priorities of digital systems to value and preserve plurality, complexity, ambiguity, and variability.
  • Socially-Oriented and Ethically-Focused: Humanistic computing should place a focus on the social, ethical, political and ecological dimensions of computing. This entails a constantly critical practice and a deliberate choice of application areas, but also, crucially, the integration of humanistic theories and frameworks, in part to help interrogate and counter a history of power and privilege.

In the framework, the 'disposition' sections describe how the activities can relate to these dimensions.

The competency framework is intended to guide instructors in the creation of competencies for their specific content that also align with the notion of humanistic computing.
