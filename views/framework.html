The structure of the competency framework is informed by the fact that the DH community has expressed that it doesn’t want a curriculum document that dictates content, as is the case of CS. Instead, the idea of this framework is that instructors can define competencies for their specific course context that exist at the nexus of activities and literacies. It is deliberately flexible; a given context may not make use of all the activities and instructors may choose to focus more on some literacies than others, depending on the aims of their programme or needs of their students.

The following sections outline sample competencies for the activities on a fairly abstract level and illustrate them using semi-concrete examples situated in the context of DH projects of different types drawn from Reviews in DH.

Ideally, users of the framework can submit their own competency examples, to eventually be integrated here as a community resource.

Competency Descriptions
Designing · Discovering
Creation
Learners are able to:
  • Identify the questions they would like to pose of a resource or that they are looking to address with a proposed tool
  • Describe how they might answer these questions using different digital representations, tools, or methods, and compare possible approaches
Mechanical · Mediatory
Learners are able to:
  • identify and describe the types of tools/technologies needed and approximate hardware and infrastructural requirements of potential paths
  • explain how the affordances of these technologies make them similar to and set them apart for existing analog methods
For a project like the Virtual Viking Longship Project, they might think about whether VR or some other form of XR is best suited to their context, consider the different types of hardware available, investigate the different existing software options, and consider the practicalities of deployment.
Procedural · Relational
Learners are able to give a high-level description of intended outcome and the network of steps required to reach it.
For a project like Affirmations, this could involve laying out the story, goals and game mechanics but also thinking through the different elements/layers (programming, writing, art, sound) and the connections/dependencies between them.
For a project like AudiAnnotate this could involve identifying all the different text analysis procedures/methods they want to support, describing the ways users will interact with each of them and breaking these down into functionality.
Functional · Rhetorical
Learners are able to justify/refute technical approaches by linking their affordances to research goals and contextually appropriate theory.
The Comédie-Française Registers Project could have chosen to take a text-forward approach to capturing grave data; a spatial approach, or even a relational/networked approach with a focus on family lines; competence in this dimension would mean being able to explain how the choice of, for instance, TEI XML or a relational database to encode information makes sense, in light of the dimensions/qualities of the data they are interested in and planned data transformations/manipulations.
Disposition Aspects
  • HHHF: Learners are explaining their choices in relation to the framing of their objects as highly contextual cultural artefacts.
  • PC: Learners are comparing multiple representations, which serves to highlight the non-neutrality and constructedness of computational objects
Modelling · Representing
Creation
Learners can create a ‘datafication’ of a physical source (a process which may include both digitisation and encoding) or create a computational representation of a problem or method/process.
Mechanical · Mediatory
Learners can:
  • understand how information is represented/stored by the machine and, at a higher level, by specific digital tools
  • explain how and why they have adapted/transformed their source/question for computational constraints/affordances.
In a project like the Comédie-Française Registers Project, which transformed a textual archive of theatre performances into relational databases, this could entail knowing about the capacities of different datatypes to figure out their suitability for different dimensions, thinking about the kind of normalization that might be needed to do aggregations but also how to preserve nuance that might be of interest, and figuring out what kinds of database relationships (e.g. one-many) fit the often irregular relationships of the data.
Procedural · Relational
Learners are able to specify/describe:
  • the mapping between problem/source elements and components of a digital model, including how the components are connected/interdependent.
  • the relationship between data and context, including questions of consent and sovereignty
For a project like the Comédie-Française Registers Project, this could look like being able to explain the entities that are included (e.g. actors, plays, dates) and how they are connected (e.g. actors performed a play on a specific date), understanding how this relationship manifests as its own object (performances), even if there is no knowledge of the specific implementation (e.g. foreign keys, unique ids).
For a project like Bukit Brown Cemetery Documentation Project, this could look like learning about culturally specific notions of sacredness and what it is respectful to collect/for certain groups to view
Functional · Rhetorical
Learners can explain the implications/justify of modelling choices (e.g. what dimensions were included/excluded, what transformations occurred, how relationships are represented) in relation to their base questions/problem and theoretical lens(es)
In the the case of the Comédie-Française Registers Project, this might look like noting cases of ambiguity (such as tickets covering an unknown number of guests that might impact aggregation), explaining the presence of additions made beyond the source text (why they are important, where they come from) and reckoning with both the limitations and affordances of the chosen encoding (e.g. created to support fine grained numeric calculation but leaves blocks of textual data nearly untreated)
In a case of a project like the Anti-Eviction Mapping Project, this might involve explaining their choice of points to include in storymaps
Disposition Aspects
  • PC: Computational encoding can resist ambiguity and variation; students should think through what parts of the complexity and multiplicity they see worth preserving and how to do so within the constraints of the medium
  • SOEF: Data can be used as a tool for oppression, with marginalised perspectives silenced by the conflation of ‘general’ and ‘dominant’. Students should be reflecting on what voices and viewpoints their encodings uplift or overlook.
Recursing · Refining
Creation
Learners are able to:
  • incrementally construct digital components for problem solving and interpretation that build upon their own work or that of others
  • modify existing methods and tools for their own context and explain the changes in terms of concrete outcome and theoretical grounding
  • situate components and identify their effect in the context of larger systems -- technical and social alike
Mechanical · Mediatory
Learners are able to:
  • render imagined processes programmatically
  • identify places where this representation differs from the initial conceptualisation
  • engage with questions of version management and compatibility
For a project like .break.dance, this means devising and implementing the mechanisms (visual behaviour, interaction mechanisms) to represent the abstract ideas like ‘hauntedness’ and ‘agency’ that they wanted to communicate.
For a project like On the Books it would involve writing, training and fine tuning the ML algorithms used for text identification and categorisation.
Procedural · Relational
Learners have an understanding of computational building blocks and pipelines; they can describe the relationships between them in terms of inputs/outputs and dependencies, including interactions with humans.
For a project like AudiAnnotate, this would entail being able to describe how audio is uploaded, broken down and annotated, and how the annotations are represented, linked to the audio, and exported.
For a project like the George Eliot Archive, this would involve being able to describe how all the different elements (types of data and interactions) as well as the different layers that compose the system (where is the data stored and it what format, how does the interface access the data, etc.) fit together.
Functional · Rhetorical
Disposition Aspects
Context Projects

The scope of DH is broad and, correspondingly, the following categorisations and examples inevitably miss some aspects of DH. They are intended only to survey some of the most common project types and, in many places, overlap. Only one or two examples are given per subcategory; this is not an assertion that all projects within a subcategory share outlooks or concerns or that the scope of the projects can be confined to a single category.

  • Investigating a textual, physical, or auditory object, source or archive
    • Digital editions: The Lady's Museum Project
    • Digital reconstructions or exhibitions: Virtual Viking Longship Project
    • Source digitisation and transformation: The Comédie-Française Registers Project (textual source), The Bukit Brown Cemetery Documentation Project (physical source)
    • Digital processing and analysis (e.g. visualisation, text analysis, network analysis, mapping): George Eliot Archive
    • Source assemblages: War History on the Semantic Web (semantic web), On the Books: Jim Crow and Algorithms of Resistance (text mining)
  • Investigating/engaging with a social and/or technical phenomenon
    • Serious Games: Affirmations
    • Data crowdsourcing and dissemination: Anti-Eviction Mapping Project, Native Land Digital
    • Creative Digital Storytelling: .break .dance
  • Developing a generic tool to address a process or method
    • Annotation: AudiAnnotate
    • Data management/transformation: Corpora
    • Analysis: Distant Viewing Toolkit
